## 1. Executive summary

1. **Every consumer AI trip planner disclaims accountability.** None that I found stands behind its itinerary. The standard approach has four parts: (a) "as is / as available" with no warranty of accuracy; (b) "we are not a travel agent, booking partner terms govern"; (c) liability capped or excluded for third-party and indirect loss; (d) support by email or chat only, with no rebooking power. Mindtrip, GuideGeek, Layla, Wanderlog, Expedia's AI terms, Kayak (terms of 1 Jul 2026) and Google AI Mode all follow some version of this [verified/reported, §2]. The newest entrant, **Booking Holdings' Lola** (launched 29 Sep 2026 by Kayak co-founders), is the first big-player AI assistant to pair a **paid membership with escalation to a human concierge** [reported].
2. **The law is moving the other way.** *Moffatt v Air Canada* (2024 BCCRT 149) held that a company cannot treat its chatbot as a separate entity and is liable for its negligent misrepresentations [verified]. The **UK CMA's guidance "Complying with consumer law when using AI agents" (9 Mar 2026)** states: "if an AI agent you use does something illegal, you are responsible … even if someone else designed or provides the AI agent on your behalf" [verified, gov.uk]. The revised EU Package Travel Directive, **Directive (EU) 2026/1024 of 29 Apr 2026**, **abolishes "linked travel arrangements"**. Services from separate traders booked through linked online processes, with personal data passed on and all contracts concluded within 24 hours, become a package. Member States have **28 months to transpose (≈ Sep 2028), with application reportedly from ≈ Mar 2029** [verified for directive; reported for dates]. **An AI planner that orchestrates multi-supplier bookings in the EU is at real risk of becoming an "organiser"**, with full performance liability and an insolvency-protection duty [inferred, lawyer needed]. The UK went the opposite way: its 2026 PTR amendments abolish LTAs **without** turning "type B" linked bookings into packages, from 6 Apr 2027 [verified].
3. **"Who pays when the agent is wrong?" is now openly unsolved, but it is becoming insurable.** Skift ("Trust fall", Mar 2026; Decision Brief, Sep 2026), Morgan Lewis (Aug 2026) and Citi (Nov 2026: AI companies should pay) all frame it [reported]. On the supply side, **Amex launched "Agent Purchase Protection" for erroneous purchases by registered AI agents (14 Apr 2026)** [verified, press release]. **Armilla (Lloyd's/Chaucer, up to $25M), Munich Re aiSure and Testudo (Lloyd's)** now write affirmative cover for AI errors and hallucinations [verified/reported]. **Maryland's new seller-of-travel law (effective 1 Oct 2026) requires ≥ $1M professional liability/E&O insurance** [verified, bill text].
4. **Real accountability today comes from humans plus money: card issuers, TMCs, human advisors and fintech guarantees.** Examples are Amex/Chase travel desks and card trip-delay insurance, Navan and Perk (ex-TravelPerk) 24/7 support, Hopper's disruption rebooking product and CFAR, Airbnb AirCover, and Booking.com's price match [prior/reported]. These are funded by margin, subscription/annual fees or ancillary premiums, not by the AI. **Warning sign:** the FTC's **$35M Hopper settlement (Jul 2026)** targeted hidden fees and a "VIP Support" promise of help "instantly or within 5–10 minutes" that consumers often could not reach [verified, ftc.gov]. **A support SLA you sell becomes a deception risk if you miss it.**
5. **Gap.** No one offers an AI planner that (a) **guarantees** that the facts in its itinerary are correct (opening hours, last cable car, visa/ESTA, transfer feasibility) and pays if they are wrong; (b) **acts as one accountable party** across all bookings; and (c) **monitors the trip and actually rebooks** during disruption, with a human who has GDS access behind it. Slicktrip summarises the current market as "AI can book your flight but not rebook it" [reported]. The 2026 record adds **AI agents making wrong *bookings***, not just giving wrong advice: Delta's AI Concierge cancelled a member's whole award trip (Aug 2026), and United's text assistant confirmed a cancellation the customer had declined; United later issued a **$200 travel certificate**, the first voluntary AI-error compensation by a travel company that I found [reported].

**Pattern [inferred].** Planners that *only plan* disclaim everything and sell nothing, so they have little to stand behind. OTAs that *sell* offer real support and some guarantees, but wall their AI off with "unverified, as-is" language. The industry has therefore split into two kinds of liability. **Transaction liability** is accepted by OTAs and suppliers. **Advice liability**, meaning the itinerary's facts, sequencing and feasibility, is accepted by nobody. Most real-world AI travel harm (§7) falls into the advice category, though 2026 adds the first **agent-action** harms (Delta, United). Lola is the first signal that an incumbent will charge for a human backstop on top of AI.

Mark Pestronk (travel-law columnist, Travel Weekly) notes that OTAs are agents of both supplier and traveller and in principle owe advisor-like duties, but they disclaim them in their terms. Agentic AI companies go further and disclaim being anyone's agent at all [reported]. Pestronk's separate column "Advice from ChatGPT is advice from you" warns human advisors that relaying AI output makes it *their* advice [reported, title only]. This is the same principle as *Moffatt*. Morgan Lewis adds that disclaimers are "less convincing when the service is marketed as a hands-free booking assistant while the terms require the traveler to independently confirm every detail" [reported].

## 3. Legal landscape

### 3.1 Chatbot liability: *Moffatt v Air Canada*, 2024 BCCRT 149 (14 Feb 2024) [verified]
- Air Canada's website chatbot told Mr Moffatt he could claim a bereavement fare retroactively. Policy said otherwise, and a linked policy page contradicted the bot.
- The BC Civil Resolution Tribunal found **negligent misrepresentation**. It rejected the argument that the chatbot was "a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions" (the adjudicator called it "a remarkable submission"), held that Air Canada failed to take reasonable care that the bot was accurate, and held that the customer's reliance was reasonable. **Linking to the correct policy did not cure the error.**
- Award: C$812.02 total (C$650.88 damages + C$36.14 interest + C$125 fees) [reported, multiple]. The case set no contractual-promise doctrine, but it is the leading cited precedent.
- **Later cases:** A 2026 re-check again found **no appellate or later court decision** on consumer chatbot liability in travel. 2026 commentary (The Conversation, law-firm notes) still treats *Moffatt* as the reference point. The nearest new litigation is non-travel: in Sep 2026 a US court let defamation claims over Google AI summaries proceed [reported]. Not exhaustive.
- **Implication [inferred].** "AI-generated, may be inaccurate" disclaimers are weak against negligent misrepresentation where the product is designed and marketed to be relied on, such as a planner that says "the last ropeway is at 17:30." In the EU and UK, unfair-terms law (Directive 93/13; UK CRA 2015 Part 2) further limits blanket exclusions in consumer contracts. Expedia's AI terms concede this ("we do not exclude our liability where we are not permitted to").

### 3.2 UK: CMA on AI agents [verified]
The CMA published **"Complying with consumer law when using AI agents" on 9 March 2026** (gov.uk), with a companion policy paper on agentic AI and consumers [verified]. Key points:
- "If an AI agent you use does something illegal, you are responsible … even if someone else designed or provides the AI agent on your behalf." The same rules apply to AI and human agents [verified, gov.uk text]. Law-firm summaries put it as "in the same way as … actions of their employees" [reported].
- Four practical steps: tell customers when an AI agent is in use; don't overstate the AI's role or capability; design/train agents to comply with consumer law; monitor performance regularly with human oversight [reported: Cooley, Taylor Wessing, TLT, Ashurst].
- The guidance is non-binding, but sits on the **DMCC Act 2024** direct-enforcement regime (since April 2025), with fines up to **10% of global turnover** [reported, Ashurst].
- **Implication [inferred]:** an AI planner selling to UK consumers is treated as having said whatever its agent said. Vendor contracts with LLM providers do not shift that liability.

### 3.3 EU Package Travel Directive (PTD) — the biggest structural risk/opportunity
- **Current law (Directive (EU) 2015/2302) [prior].** An "organiser" combining at least 2 types of travel service (transport, accommodation, car rental, other tourist services) for the same trip is **liable for performance of all services** (Art 13), whoever supplies them. The organiser must give price-reduction or damages remedies, provide assistance to travellers in difficulty (Art 16), and hold **insolvency protection** (Art 17). "Click-through" packages already exist where traveller data passes between traders and the second contract is concluded within 24 hours (Art 3(2)(b)(v)). "Linked travel arrangements" (LTAs) carried lighter duties: insolvency protection for payments received, plus information.
- **Revision status.** Commission proposal Nov 2023. **Provisional Council–Parliament agreement 2 Dec 2025** [verified, Consilium]. **EP plenary approval 12 Mar 2026 (537–2–24)** [verified, Agence Europe/Legislative Train]. Council adoption reported 30 Mar 2026 [reported]. **Directive (EU) 2026/1024 of 29 Apr 2026** amends 2015/2302 [verified in OJ by first pass; confirmed by Garrigues, CMS, ECC Spain]. Entry into force is reported as **28 May 2026** (Garrigues) or 5 May 2026 (one source); **transposition 28 months (≈ Sep 2028); application reportedly ≈ Mar 2029** [reported; conflicting; check OJ, **lawyer needed**]. Key changes [reported]:
  - **LTAs abolished.** Services bought from separate traders through **linked online booking processes**, where personal data moves between traders and **all contracts are concluded within 24 hours**, are **a package** (new Art 3(2)(2)(b)(v)).
  - A **package definition "adapted to online booking platforms"**, plus new **information duties** where no package arises.
  - **14-day refunds** for cancelled packages; vouchers voluntary, and travellers may refuse them; insolvency protection extends to outstanding refund claims and unredeemed vouchers.
  - **How platforms are caught (CMS analysis) [reported].** CMS titles its notes "the PTD is no longer really about package holidays; it is increasingly about who controls the customer journey" and "Digital platforms are redefining package travel liability." It lists **cross-selling, click-through journeys, integrated payment processes and personalised recommendations** as the factors that decide whether a business becomes the organiser. It warns that "for travel platforms, liability may arise much earlier than expected," making package liability "a business and product-design issue." No commentary found specifically analyses AI agents, and no "facilitator" category was found in the final text.
- **What this means for an AI planner [inferred, lawyer needed]:**
  - If the planner **takes payment or passes traveller data to a second trader** (for example, booking a flight and then pushing the traveller's details to a hotel API) within 24h, it is likely the **organiser** of a package. That carries strict performance liability, an insolvency-protection requirement and a duty to assist.
  - Pure **referral/deep-link** models where the traveller re-enters data at each supplier are the classic way to avoid package status. Most AI planners do this today (Mindtrip, Google AI Mode, ChatGPT apps). Note that CMS flags *personalised recommendations* as a factor, so even "referral" models should be reviewed.
  - "Agentic" one-click multi-booking is precisely the conduct the revision targets. A product that bundles bookings to be "the accountable one" must either **embrace organiser status** (with bonding/insurance, which is how tour operators, Tourlane and similar work) or deliberately structure flows to stay outside it. The ~2028–29 application date gives roughly 2 years to design for it.

### 3.4 UK ATOL & Package Travel Regulations [verified/reported]
- **ATOL.** Selling flight-inclusive packages to UK consumers requires an **ATOL** from the CAA, with bonding/financial tests and Air Travel Trust coverage. The **ATOL Protection Contribution (APC) is still £2.50 per passenger**, paid by ATOL holders to the Air Travel Trust [verified, CAA FAQ; ATIPAC 2025/26 annual report, Jun 2026]. ATOL reform is **still undecided**: a CAA consultation asked whether the APC should be risk-adjusted (NI Travel News: could rise to £15/person under one proposal), and the CAA's June 2026 ATIPAC report lists "a decision on ATOL Reform" as an open industry ask [verified/reported]. The ATT fund reached a record ~£355m [reported]. Agents who sell as **agent for an ATOL holder** use an "Agent for ATOL holder" arrangement [prior].
- **PTRs.** DBT's consultation "Package travel – updating the framework 2025" (7 Apr–30 Jun 2025) led to the **Package Travel and Linked Travel Arrangements (Amendment) Regulations 2026 (SI 2026/455)**, debated in the Lords on 21 Apr 2026 [verified]. Changes [verified/reported: legislation.gov.uk, ABTA, Weightmans, DWF]:
  - **LTAs abolished.** LTA "type A" (separate services sold via a single trader at one point of contact) becomes a **package**. "Type B" (targeted linked booking via a second trader) is **removed entirely, with no protection**. This is the *opposite* of the EU direction on linked online bookings.
  - Organisers get clearer **redress rights against suppliers** (airlines, hotels).
  - Applies to contracts made **on or after 6 April 2027**.
  - Domestic-package exemption: 65% of consultees supported it, but I found **no evidence it was enacted** [unconfirmed].
  - Insolvency-protection/ATOL interface fixes deferred to longer-term reform. AITO called the package "disappointing … a missed opportunity" [reported].
- **Implication [inferred, lawyer needed]:** in the UK from April 2027, an AI planner that books flight + hotel through **one checkout** is an organiser (and needs ATOL for flights). One that **hands off** to a second trader's checkout carries lighter duties than in the EU.

US federal law adds several layers. ### 3.6 EU AI Act [verified/reported]
- Travel planners are **not high-risk** under Annex III. **Art 50(1)** obliges providers to ensure people are informed they are interacting with an AI system. **It applied from 2 Aug 2026 and was not postponed** by the AI Omnibus [reported, Gibson Dunn, Lewis Silkin]. **Art 50(2)** (machine-readable marking of synthetic content) applies from 2 Aug 2026 for new systems; systems already on the market before then have until **2 Dec 2026** [reported]. The **AI Omnibus** was agreed 7 May 2026, cleared by the Council ~9 Jul 2026, **published in the OJ 24 Jul 2026 and in force 27 Jul 2026** [reported, FPF/Garrigues/Cuatrecasas]. Annex III high-risk obligations moved to **2 Dec 2027**, Annex I to **2 Aug 2028**; new Art 5 prohibitions apply from 2 Dec 2026 [reported].
- Edge case: **AI-driven risk assessment/pricing for life and health insurance** is high-risk (Annex III 5(c)). Embedding travel *medical* insurance with AI underwriting or pricing could arguably be caught (now from Dec 2027). **Lawyer needed.**
- The **AI Liability Directive** was withdrawn (Commission 2025 work programme) [prior]. The **new Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853** explicitly covers **software including AI**. It must be transposed by **9 Dec 2026** and applies to products placed on the market after that. It covers death or personal injury, property damage and data loss, **but not pure economic loss** [prior]. A hallucinated trail that injures someone is in scope. A missed non-refundable hotel is not, and stays under contract/negligence/consumer law.

### 3.7 Passenger rights: what AI can claim for users
- **EU261/2004 (and UK261).** €250/€400/€600 compensation for delays ≥3h at arrival, cancellations with <14 days' notice, or denied boarding, unless "extraordinary circumstances" apply. Also duty of care (meals, hotel) and the right to refund or re-routing [prior; still current law]. **Reform now adopted.** The Council's June 2025 position (4h/6h thresholds, €300/€500) was resisted by Parliament. Conciliation produced a deal on **15 Jun 2026**; EP approved 7 Jul 2026 (646–12–3); **Council gave final clearance 13 Jul 2026** [verified, Consilium]. Most sources say the **3h threshold and €250–600 amounts were kept** (one outlier says 4h/6h) [reported; check final text]. The Council says the rules apply **12 months + 20 days after OJ publication**; one law-firm alert gives **Regulation (EU) 2026/2202, in force 22 Oct 2026, applying from 23 Oct 2027** [reported, single source]. Notable content [verified/reported]:
  - Ban on denying a return flight because the outbound was not used.
  - Tighter "extraordinary circumstances" definition; their mere occurrence no longer automatically exempts the airline.
  - **Claims mechanics:** a single EU-wide **9-month** claim deadline; airlines must acknowledge and **pay or reasoned-reject within 30 days**; airlines must send passengers a **link to the claim form within 48h or 96h** of scheduled arrival (sources conflict). Earlier ideas for pre-filled forms and automatic payouts were **reportedly dropped**. Commentators expect claim agencies' "information-asymmetry" business to shrink [reported: AirAdvisor, Burges Salmon, OMAAT, ITIJ].
- **US DOT.** The **automatic refund rule** (Apr 2024; refunds within 7 business days by card, 20 calendar days otherwise, for cancellations and significant changes) remains in force; core duties are also codified in the FAA Reauthorization Act 2024 [reported]. DOT is pursuing a deregulatory **"Airline Refunds and Other Consumer Protections III"** rulemaking (RIN 2105-AF36) to "reduce burdens", including redefining cancellations for renumbered flights. Its enforcement pause on renumbered flights runs to **7 Jul 2027** (FR 2026-13675) [verified, Federal Register; reported, Cozen O'Connor]. The **cash-compensation-for-controllable-delays ANPRM (Dec 2024) was withdrawn**: announced Sep 2025, formalised in the Federal Register ~late Nov 2025, on grounds of regulatory burden and doubtful legal authority. Bills to legislate compensation have been introduced [reported, Eckert Seamans, NBC]. DOT's airline Customer Service Dashboard lists airline *commitments* (meals, hotel vouchers) that are enforceable as representations [prior].
- **Canada APPR.** Amended regime in force Sept 2023; compensation C$125–1,000 [prior].
- **Automation opportunity [inferred].** Most of this is mechanically determinable: route, carrier, actual arrival time (FlightAware/Cirium/OAG), cause codes (weak; airlines dispute them). Claim services (AirHelp, Flightright) charge about 30–35% + VAT, with extra legal-action fees [prior]. A planner that **files EU261/DOT/APPR claims for free, inside the subscription,** is a concrete accountability feature that costs little and is visible to users. The reformed EU261 (airline must send a claim link; 30-day answer; 9-month window) makes in-app filing *easier* and squeezes paid claim agencies, so a free filer is better placed from late 2027.

**Lessons [inferred]:**
1. **Accountability that works is pre-funded.** It comes from a fee (Hopper, Perk), an annual card fee (Amex/Chase), service fees (Airbnb), membership (Lola) or insurance premiums. Nobody funds it out of an LLM subscription.
2. **The best experiences combine a guarantee with execution power.** Hopper's product matters because Hopper *rebooks* (up to $2,000 on any airline). A refund promise without rebooking capability is half a solution. Hopper's partial-refund alternative shows the guarantee is priced, not unlimited.
3. **Humans remain the escalation backstop.** Airbnb, with the best AI support resolution rate reported, still resolves at most about 45%. It explicitly preserves "accurate" escalation for time-critical and safety issues. Lola's design (AI + paid human concierge) confirms the pattern. Klarna's 2025 partial reversal back toward human agents is a cautionary data point [prior].
4. **Published response times are a liability, not just marketing.** Navan and Perk publish targets, not SLAs. Hopper's "instant / 5–10 minute" VIP Support promise became part of an FTC deception case. **Any SLA must be one you can staff 24/7, with remedies defined if you miss it.**

## 9. Positioning, mechanisms, business models, risks

### 9.1 Unoccupied positioning
**"The AI planner that stands behind its itinerary."** Concretely:
1. **Verified itinerary.** Every load-bearing fact (entry docs, opening hours, last departures, transfer times, closures) is checked against authoritative sources and time-stamped, with a visible "verified at T" badge. Unverified items are visibly flagged.
2. **Itinerary Guarantee.** If a *verified* fact is wrong and it costs you money (missed ropeway, closed attraction, wrong visa document), we reimburse up to $X. Insurance backs this, now feasible via aiSure-style performance warranties or Armilla/Lloyd's-style AI cover.
3. **Trip watch + act.** 24/7 monitoring of flights and bookings. On disruption, the AI proposes and executes a re-plan, and a human with GDS access handles what the AI can't, with a published response SLA (only one you can meet; see the Hopper FTC case).
4. **Claims on autopilot.** EU261/UK261/DOT refunds/APPR and insurance claims are filed for the user at **0% commission** (included in the plan). Parametric delay cover pays automatically.

### 9.3 Business models that can fund real accountability
1. **Subscription concierge tier** (e.g. $10–30/month or per-trip $49–149). Funds 24/7 human escalation, claims filing and the accuracy guarantee. Analogues: Amex/Chase annual fees, Navan/Perk SaaS, Fora Pro, **Lola's paid membership** [inferred pricing].
2. **Merchant-of-record margin** on hotels and activities (merchant rates often 15–25%+ vs 10–15% agency commissions) [prior, approximate]. Funds service but triggers seller-of-travel (incl. Maryland E&O), PTD and chargeback exposure.
3. **Ancillary/fintech products** (disruption guarantee, CFAR, price freeze): Hopper's model, high-margin if priced well. **The FTC's $35M Hopper settlement** (hidden/pre-selected fees, "deposit" framing, unmet support promises) and the FTC fees rule show the design constraints.
4. **Insurance distribution commission** (often 20–40% of premium) [prior, approximate] plus **parametric delay cover**. Natural fit because the planner holds itinerary and flight data.
5. **Claims share**, as a fallback for litigated claims only. Keep simple claims free to differentiate from AirHelp-type fees; the EU261 reform erodes agencies' information advantage anyway.
6. **B2B2C**: sell the "accountable planner" to card issuers and banks, which already fund 24/7 travel service and want to push agent liability onto someone (the Citi stance; Amex agent purchase protection) [inferred].

### 9.4 Legal risks of standing behind the itinerary (lawyer needed on each)
- **Package organiser status (EU/UK).** In the EU, bundled booking flows plus data passing within 24h make a package under Directive (EU) 2026/1024 (national laws ~Sep 2028, application ~2029). That means strict liability for all components, an insolvency-protection requirement and a duty to assist. CMS flags personalised recommendations and integrated payments as factors. In the UK, single-trader bundles are packages from 6 Apr 2027 (SI 2026/455).
- **ATOL (UK)** for flight-inclusive packages (APC £2.50/passenger; reform pending, possibly risk-based and higher).
- **US seller-of-travel registration/bonding/trust accounts** (CA, FL, WA, HI, IA) and, **new from 1 Oct 2026, Maryland: registration + ≥ $1M PL/E&O insurance**, with no explicit platform exemption found.
- **Insurance regulation.** An "accuracy guarantee" or "disruption guarantee" may be characterised as **insurance**, requiring an insurer partner (the planner as distributor/agent) rather than self-underwriting. Insurance distribution requires licensing (EU IDD, US state producer licences, UK FCA).
- **Advertising/deception (FTC §5, UK DMCC/CPRs, EU UCPD).** "Guaranteed," "verified," "AI travel agent" and response-time claims must match capability. Precedents: FTC Operation AI Comply; **FTC v. Hopper (2026)** on support-speed promises and fee presentation; CMA "don't overstate the AI's role" step. Display all-in prices (FTC fees rule for lodging). (DOT's airline ancillary-fee disclosure rule is gone, but §41712 unfair/deceptive practices still applies to ticket agents.)
- **Responsibility for agents (UK CMA; *Moffatt*).** The business answers for whatever its AI agent says or does, even if the vendor built it. Raising the reliance users place on you increases exposure. The guarantee should be the **exclusive remedy up to a cap** where law permits, with consumer-law floors preserved.
- **Unfair contract terms.** Blanket AI disclaimers are partly unenforceable for EU/UK consumers.
- **Product Liability Directive (from 9 Dec 2026).** Personal-injury harms from AI software (dangerous routes, wrong safety info) fall under strict liability. Pure financial loss does not.
- **EU AI Act Art 50(1)** disclosure (applying since 2 Aug 2026); Art 50(2) marking for synthetic content (Dec 2026 for systems already on the market). High-risk only if doing AI pricing/underwriting for health/life insurance (from Dec 2027).
- **Data protection.** Monitoring trips and passing traveller data to suppliers is a GDPR processing chain, and the same data passing helps create package status under the revised PTD.
- **Chargeback / payment-network rules for agent-initiated transactions.** Network frameworks (Visa TAP, Mastercard Agent Pay, Amex ACE) give identity and intent records but not a liability allocation beyond fraud. As merchant of record, the planner bears chargebacks by default, and agent disputes count toward Visa monitoring thresholds. Issuers (Citi) signal that the agent operator should bear "unintended purchase" losses.
